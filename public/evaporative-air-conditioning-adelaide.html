The basics

What is evaporative air conditioning?

Evaporative air conditioning cools a home by drawing hot outside air through water-soaked pads in a roof-mounted unit. As the water evaporates it absorbs heat, and a fan pushes the cooled air through ducts to ceiling outlets in each room. Stale indoor air leaves through open windows or doors, so the house is constantly refreshed.

There's no refrigerant and no compressor. The power goes into a fan and a small water pump, which is why it uses so little electricity. The Australian Government's Your Home guide makes the same point: only the fan and pump use energy.

The trade-off is that evaporative cooling depends on the weather. It works best when the outside air is hot and dry, which describes most Adelaide summer days, and it struggles when the air is already humid.

Inside the system

How ducted evaporative cooling works

  • 1Cooling pads. Absorbent pads on each side of the roof unit, kept wet while the system runs.
  • 2Water pump and reservoir. The pump lifts water from a tray at the base of the unit over the pads. A float valve tops it up from the mains.
  • 3Fan. Pulls outside air through the wet pads and pushes it down into the ducts. It does most of the work, and uses most of the power.
  • 4Ductwork. Carries cooled air from the roof unit across the ceiling space.
  • 5Ceiling outlets. Release cool air into each room. There are no return air grilles, because the air isn't recirculated.
  • 6Open windows or doors. Give the warm, stale air somewhere to go. Without them the system pressurises the house and cooling stalls.

One more part worth knowing: the drain or dump valve. As water evaporates, the minerals it carried stay behind. The unit periodically drains some water from the reservoir to stop them building up as scale on the pads, and the drain valve is one of the first things we test on a service.

Getting the most from it

How to use evaporative cooling properly

Most complaints about evaporative cooling come down to how it's run, not the unit. Refrigerated air conditioning needs a sealed house. Evaporative needs the opposite. The South Australian Government puts it plainly: evaporative systems are more effective with some doors and windows open.

  • Open a window or door in each room you want cooled. A gap of a few centimetres is usually enough.
  • Close windows in rooms you aren't using, so the cool air is pulled towards the rooms you are.
  • Turn it on early on a hot day, before the house has soaked up heat.
  • On a humid day, switch to fan only. It still moves air, without adding moisture.
  • When outside air quality is poor, such as bushfire smoke, limit use. Your Home makes the same recommendation.

How many windows should be open with evaporative cooling?

Enough to let the stale air out. The South Australian Government says evaporative systems work better with some doors and windows open. Open a window or door in each room you are cooling, a few centimetres is usually enough, and close windows in rooms you are not using so the cool air goes where you want it.

Winter tip. A roof unit is a large opening in your ceiling. Your Home notes built-in evaporative coolers can increase heating bills by letting warm air escape, and recommends closing off ducts and covering the roof unit in winter unless the unit has automatic seals.

What is a downside of evaporative cooling?

It cools less on humid days, needs some windows or doors open, uses water and does not heat. The Your Home guide notes central systems can use 25 litres or more an hour on hot, dry days, and roof units leak warm air in winter unless sealed. In bushfire areas the unit needs an ember screen.

The honest trade-offs

Downsides of evaporative cooling

  • Humid days. Evaporation slows when the air already holds moisture, so cooling drops. The SA Government says refrigerated systems are the better choice in humid conditions.
  • Water use. Your Home puts central systems at 25 litres or more an hour on hot, dry days, which matters during water restrictions.
  • No heating. You'll need a separate heater for Adelaide winters.
  • Winter heat loss. Without automatic seals or a winter cover, warm air escapes through the roof unit.
  • Outside air quality. The system brings outside air straight in, including smoke and dust on bad days.
  • Bushfire risk. The CFA warns roof units can catch fire from embers. An ember screen is the most effective protection.
Servicing

Evaporative air conditioning service in Adelaide

How often should an evaporative air conditioner be serviced?

At least once a year, ideally in spring before the first heatwave. Some manufacturers also require a service at set intervals to keep a new-unit warranty valid, so it is worth checking your paperwork. Homes in dusty areas, or units that run most days, benefit from extra pad and water reservoir cleaning.

What an evaporative service covers

  • Pads inspected for scale, dirt and wear, cleaned or replaced
  • Water reservoir drained, cleaned and refilled
  • Pump tested and water flow over every pad checked
  • Float valve and water supply line checked for leaks
  • Drain or dump valve tested so minerals don't build up
  • Fan, motor and belt (on older units) checked
  • Ceiling outlets and accessible ductwork inspected
  • Controller and thermostat tested
  • Winter seal or damper checked, and ember screen if fitted

What should I do with my evaporative cooler in a bushfire?

Fit an ember screen well before the fire season. The CFA advises running the unit to keep the pads wet when smoke is nearby, turning it off once smoke is overhead or ash is falling, and checking the unit for embers when it is safe. External screens should have openings no wider than 2 mm.

Hills and fringe suburbs

Evaporative coolers and bushfire safety

For homes near the Adelaide Hills or other bushfire-prone areas, the roof unit needs attention before summer. The CFA's guidance on evaporative coolers recommends:

  • An external, non-combustible ember screen over the air intakes, made of corrosion-resistant steel, bronze or aluminium
  • Mesh or perforated sheet with openings no larger than 2 mm
  • Small gaps sealed with fire-resistant foam or sealer
  • Screens fitted well before the fire season

Evaporative air conditioning FAQs

Can an evaporative cooler cool an entire house?

A ducted evaporative system can. One roof unit feeds ceiling outlets throughout the house, and the South Australian Government lists ducted evaporative systems as a whole-home cooling option for the state's dry climate. A portable evaporative cooler suits a single room.

Does evaporative cooling use gas or electricity?

Electricity and water. The fan and water pump run on electricity, and the pads use mains water. There is no gas and no refrigerant.

Does evaporative cooling work when it rains?

It runs, but it cools less. Rain and storms raise humidity, and the SA Government notes evaporative systems are less effective when it's humid. Switching to fan only on those days still moves air through the house.

What should I do with the cooler over winter?

Drain the water, turn off the water supply to the unit if it has no automatic drain, and close the ceiling outlets or fit a winter cover unless your unit has automatic seals. Your Home recommends this to cut winter heat loss.
